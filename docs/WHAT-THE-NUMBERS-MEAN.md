# What each number means — written for someone with no medical training

> **Everything below describes the synthetic demonstration case that ships with
> this repository.** The values are invented — see `docs/SYNTHETIC-DATA.md`.
> Nobody's real results are published here. It is written as though about a real
> patient because that is the only honest way to show what the tool produces,
> and because the wording is the deliverable: this is the register the dashboard
> uses when it explains a real report to a real family, on their own machine.

**This describes what was measured. It is not a diagnosis, not a prediction, and
not medical advice.** Only a treating team can say what numbers mean for a
person, because they can also examine them and know what treatment is running.
Numbers alone never tell the whole story.

One thing to hold on to while reading: **"outside normal" is not the same as
"getting worse."** Several numbers below are far outside normal and *improving*.
Direction over days usually matters more than any single value.

---

## The shape of the week

The liver does several jobs at once. Read together, these tests say the same
thing from different angles: **the liver is badly damaged and struggling to do
its jobs.** Around that, three stories run in different directions.

| | Direction across the eight days |
|---|---|
| **Better** | Oxygen (58 → 97), lactate (3.2 → 1.5), sodium (130 → 136), clotting time (INR 3.18 → 2.41), one infection marker (procalcitonin 4.11 → 2.06) |
| **Worse** | Urea (46 → 96), creatinine (1.74 → 2.58), ammonia (64 → 79), white cell count (11,800 → 19,100), bilirubin (14.6 → 18.0) |
| **Roughly steady** | Platelets (148 → 131), haemoglobin (11.8 → 11.2) |

That mixture is why the picture is not simply "improving" or "deteriorating".
The most useful thing to do with this page is ask the team about the two things
moving the wrong way fastest: **the kidneys and the ammonia.**

## The liver's own numbers

### Bilirubin — 14.6 → 18.0 mg/dL *(normal 0.3–1.2)*

**What it is.** A yellow-orange waste pigment made when old red blood cells are
broken down. A healthy liver pulls it out of the blood and sends it to the gut.

**What it means here.** About **fifteen times the top of normal**, and drifting
up. When bilirubin builds up it stains the skin and the whites of the eyes
yellow — jaundice, the most visible outward sign of what is happening inside.
The laboratory marked it a **critical result**, meaning it is telephoned to the
doctors rather than simply filed.

### Albumin — 2.7 g/dL *(normal 3.5–5.2)*

**What it is.** The main protein the liver *manufactures*. Think of the liver as
a factory; albumin is its flagship product.

**What it means here.** Low. This is a direct measure of whether the factory is
still working, and it says production has fallen. Albumin also holds fluid
inside blood vessels, so when it drops, fluid leaks into the belly and legs —
which is why swelling appears.

### AST 312 → 268 U/L *(normal under 50)* and ALT 96 → 81 U/L *(normal under 45)*

**What they are.** Enzymes that live *inside* liver cells. Finding a lot of them
loose in the blood means liver cells have been breaking open and spilling their
contents.

**What it means here.** AST is roughly six times the ceiling — active, ongoing
injury, not old scarring sitting quietly. Both are **falling**, which is the
first encouraging thing on this page.

## Clotting — how well blood can seal a wound

### INR — 3.18 → 2.51 → 2.28 → 2.41 *(normal under 1.3)*

**What it is.** The liver makes the proteins that let blood clot. INR expresses
how long blood takes to clot as a ratio, so any laboratory's result can be
compared with any other.

**What it means here.** At 3.18 the blood was taking over three times as long as
normal to clot. It **improved to 2.28** and then drifted slightly back. Still
well above normal, which means raised bruising and bleeding risk — and is why
nurses press longer on needle sites.

INR is also one of the four numbers that drives the MELD score below.

### Platelets — 148 → 131 thousand *(normal 150–410)*

**What they are.** The cell fragments that plug a leak and stop bleeding.

**What it means here.** Just below normal and drifting down. Combined with the
raised INR, two of the body's bleeding defences are weakened at once.

---

## Ammonia — the number that affects the mind

### Ammonia — 64 → 72 → 79 µmol/L *(normal 0–54)*

**What it is.** A waste product made when the body breaks down protein. Normally
the liver converts it into something harmless that leaves in urine.

**What it means here.** The liver is not clearing it, so it is building up — and
it **rose at every measurement**. This one matters to families more than most,
because ammonia affects the brain. It is the usual explanation when someone with
liver failure becomes confused, unusually sleepy, muddles words, or is hard to
rouse.

If that is happening, it is **a physical effect of a chemical in the blood** —
not confusion in the ordinary sense, and not something the person is choosing or
failing at. That distinction matters enormously to families and is worth saying
plainly.

One honest caution: ammonia levels and how drowsy someone actually appears do
not track each other neatly. The bedside picture matters more than the number.

## The kidneys — the clearest thing moving the wrong way

### Creatinine — 1.74 → 1.42 → 1.51 → 1.66 → **2.58** mg/dL *(normal 0.67–1.17)*

**What it is.** A waste product from normal muscle activity, cleared by the
kidneys. If it piles up, the kidneys are not filtering properly.

**What it means here.** It improved early in the week and then **rose sharply**
to more than twice the upper limit. This is the most concerning single trend.

### Urea — 46 → 55 → 61 → 78 → 96 mg/dL *(normal 13–43)*

Another waste product the kidneys remove. It **rose at every single check,
roughly doubling**. Creatinine and urea rising together is a consistent signal
that kidney function is falling.

**Why this matters in liver disease.** Severe liver failure commonly drags the
kidneys down with it — circulation changes starve the kidneys of blood flow even
when the kidneys themselves are structurally fine. Doctors watch for this
closely because it changes treatment. Worth asking about directly.

### Sodium — 130 → 136 mEq/L *(normal 136–145)*

Blood salt. It started low, which is common in advanced liver disease because
the body holds too much water and dilutes it. **It came back into normal range**
— a real improvement, and one that counts, because low sodium adds to confusion
and feeds into the MELD score.

### Phosphorus — 1.4 → **1.1** → 3.8 → 5.2 mg/dL *(normal 2.5–4.5)*

Started **critically low**, then rose above normal. Both directions are
treatable and both are watched; the swing usually reflects nutrition being
restarted and then the kidneys not clearing it.

### Potassium 3.7 → 4.3 · Chloride 98 → 105 · Calcium 8.3 → 7.9 · Magnesium 2.5 → 2.6

Salts that must stay within narrow limits for the heart, nerves and muscles to
work. None is dramatically off. These are what drips and supplements get
adjusted against.

## Infection — and why the signals disagree

This is the genuinely mixed part, and worth understanding, because a family told
"the infection marker is improving" and separately "the white count is climbing"
will reasonably be confused. Both are true.

### Procalcitonin — 4.11 → 2.06 ng/mL *(normal under 0.5)*

**What it is.** The most specific routine blood marker for **bacterial**
infection. Above 2 suggests severe infection through the body; above 10 suggests
septic shock.

**What it means here.** It **halved**. That is a meaningful improvement and
usually means antibiotics are working.

### White blood cell count — 11,800 → 9,900 → 14,600 → 19,100 cells/cumm *(normal 4,000–10,000)*

**What it is.** The infection-fighting cells.

**What it means here.** Dipped into normal, then **climbed to nearly twice the
top of normal**. Taken alone this looks like worsening infection.

### Neutrophils 78.4% → 89.6% · Lymphocytes 11.2% → 5.2% · Ratio 7.0 → 17.2

The white cells are not just more numerous — their *mix* has shifted hard toward
the emergency-response type, while the type handling longer-term immunity has
collapsed. That ratio more than doubled. In liver failure a rising ratio tracks
the body being under severe strain.

### CRP 58.2 mg/L *(normal under 10)* · Interleukin-6 142 pg/mL *(normal under 7)*

General inflammation markers, both high. These rise with inflammation from **any**
cause, including the failing liver itself — not only infection.

**How to hold these together.** The marker most specific to bacteria is
improving, while the general inflammation and white-cell signals worsen. The
likely readings are either that a bacterial infection is being controlled while
the body's overall inflammatory state still escalates from the liver injury, or
that something new is developing. **This is exactly the sort of thing to ask the
team about** — and it is the discordance the dashboard flags.

## Blood counts

### Haemoglobin 11.8 → 11.2 g/dL *(normal 13–17)*

The substance in red cells that carries oxygen. Mild anaemia, roughly stable
rather than falling sharply — reassuring, because it suggests no rapid bleeding.
Anaemia contributes to tiredness and breathlessness.

---

## Blood gas — oxygen and acid balance

Taken from an artery rather than a vein, usually in intensive care.

### Oxygen (pO2) — 58 → 97 mmHg *(normal 80–100)*

Started **critically low** — dangerously little oxygen in the blood — and
**recovered into the normal range**, usually reflecting oxygen or breathing
support working. A clear improvement.

### Lactate — 3.2 → 1.5 mmol/L *(normal 0.5–2.2)*

Accumulates when tissues are not getting enough oxygen, or when the liver cannot
clear it. Raised at the start, **ended inside normal**. Together with the oxygen
recovery, this suggests tissues were better supplied by the end of the week.

### Bicarbonate 24.1 → 21.8 · Base excess +0.4 → −2.7

The blood's acid buffer, slowly consumed, with base excess drifting negative. In
plain terms: **the body is holding its acid balance steady, but working harder
to do it.**

## The overall severity score

**MELD-Na, day by day: 36 · 36 · 32 · 31 · 30 · 31 · 32 · 36**

MELD is a single number from 6 to 40, calculated from bilirubin, INR, creatinine
and sodium. It was designed to rank how urgently someone needs a liver
transplant.

The score improved through mid-week and then climbed back — driven mainly by the
kidney numbers rising, since creatinine is one of its four inputs.

Two honest caveats. This score is calculated *from the same blood tests above*,
so it adds no new information — it summarises. And it is a statistical tool for
comparing groups of patients: it describes severity, it does not predict what
will happen to one person.

**Child-Pugh and AARC are deliberately left blank.** They need a bedside
assessment of alertness and confusion (and for Child-Pugh, how much fluid has
collected in the abdomen) that no blood test provides. Rather than guess, the
dashboard says what is missing.

## Reasonable questions for the medical team

Grounded in what these numbers actually show:

1. Urea has roughly doubled and creatinine has risen sharply. What is being done
   about the kidneys, and is dialysis being considered?
2. Ammonia has risen at every check. Is there any confusion or drowsiness, and
   is treatment for that being given?
3. Procalcitonin has halved but the white cell count has climbed. How do you
   read that?
4. AST, INR, oxygen and lactate have all improved. Are those the things you
   would call encouraging too?
5. What are you watching most closely over the next 48 hours?

## What this page cannot tell you

- **No imaging.** No scans of the liver, abdomen or chest.
- **No examination.** Nothing about how the person looks, their blood pressure,
  temperature, breathing, alertness, or how much fluid has collected.
- **No treatment record.** No medicines, drips, antibiotics, oxygen or
  procedures — so it cannot tell you what is *causing* an improvement.
- **No cause.** Nothing here says why the liver failed.
- **No prognosis.** Nothing in these numbers predicts an outcome for an
  individual, and anyone claiming otherwise from a blood test alone is
  overreaching.

The people who can put all of that together are the team at the bedside. This
page exists to help follow what they are saying and ask better questions — not
to replace them.
